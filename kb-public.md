# Celestial Design System

---

## Quick Facts

- **Timeframe:** March 2024 – October 2024
- **Company:** Comcast
- **Team:** 1 design lead, 3 designers, accessibility team, engineers
- **My Role:** Principal Experience Designer
- **Tools:** Figma, Jira, Confluence, Storybook, Airtable
- **Key deliverables:** Component creation, pattern documentation, accessibility improvements, process refinement

---

## Project Overview

Celestial is Comcast's next-generation employee-facing design system, unifying the company's frontline tools into a single, cohesive experience. Built to replace legacy 360 products, Celestial provides the shared foundation of components, patterns, and guidelines that power the entire Celestial product suite.

As a hybrid system, Celestial supports multiple platforms:

- iOS hybrid (Capacitor)
- iPadOS hybrid (Capacitor)
- Web

The design system encompasses foundations, components, and patterns that serve as the backbone for products across the Celestial ecosystem — including the Xfinity App, Flex, TV, Web/Mobile Native, and all Celestial Spaces.

Think Company was brought in to staff the CDS team. I owned the design system end-to-end — creating and documenting new components, refining existing ones, improving processes, and collaborating across departments to ensure accessibility and scalability.

---

## Problem

When I joined, the Celestial product was approximately 20% complete. While the design system had been established, it faced significant challenges:

- A vast backlog of components and patterns still needed to be designed and documented
- The system lacked the scalability and alignment needed across multiple product teams
- There was no structured process for component creation, review, and handoff
- Accessibility compliance was severely lacking — legacy components sat at roughly 15% compliance
- Collaboration between design, accessibility, and engineering teams needed to be streamlined

---

## Design Process

Each component and pattern was designed to work across four breakpoints:

- Desktop
- Tablet horizontal
- Tablet vertical
- Native mobile

The work spanned both the web UI and native app experiences. Our process was broken into three key phases.

### Phase 1: Exploration and Ideation

- Conducted research with designers across Celestial product teams to understand needs and pain points
- Performed competitive analysis to identify industry-standard patterns and best practices
- Collaborated with accessibility and engineering teams early to establish feasibility and compliance requirements
- Established the foundation for scalable, reusable components that could serve the entire product ecosystem

### Phase 2: Design

- Created components iteratively, incorporating feedback from cross-functional partners at each stage
- Maintained regular collaboration with the CDS team, Prism (the engineering counterpart), and the accessibility team
- Conducted cross-functional validation to ensure components met the needs of all consuming product teams
- Focused on improving consistency, usability, and accessibility with every iteration

### Phase 3: Delivery and Implementation

- Produced comprehensive documentation for every component, including:
  - Anatomy breakdowns
  - Behavior specifications
  - Usage guidelines
  - Accessibility requirements
  - Sticker sheets for designer consumption
- Conducted post-sprint reviews with other Pods to gather feedback and ensure adoption readiness
- Handed off completed components to Prism for integration into the shared UI kit
- Streamlined the design-to-development handoff process, reducing ambiguity and rework

---

## Outcomes

- Raised accessibility compliance from **15% (legacy) to 95%** across all new and updated components
- Standardized the component creation and documentation process, establishing a repeatable workflow adopted by the full CDS team
- Strengthened design-development collaboration through structured handoffs, shared reviews, and ongoing Prism partnership
- Facilitated seamless adoption of new components by various product Pods across the Celestial ecosystem

---

## Reflections

Celestial was an ambitious, highly collaborative effort that required balancing the needs of multiple stakeholder groups while maintaining rigorous accessibility and design standards. The work reinforced my expertise in design systems at scale, accessibility-first design, cross-functional process optimization, and building the kind of structured workflows that allow a design system to grow sustainably. It remains one of the most impactful systems-level projects in my career.

## Project Overview

The ActiveCore project is a multi-year, ongoing, dedicated and embedded project with a mix of Think and Comcast designers. The design team consisted of a Comcast design manager and a rotation of 2-3 visual and UX designers.

### Overview of the Product

ActiveCore is a network engineering platform that allows enterprise Comcast Business customers to monitor and manage their software-defined wide access network (SDWAN). I arrived on the team just before its initial release, and worked alongside 2 other designers to push the product beyond its MVP phase.

### The Challenge

Take revolutionary SDWAN technology and put it into the hands of customers in a digestible, easy to understand way, without them having to be certified IT specialists. And at the same time, find ways to include comprehensive network configuration tools that would typically require an engineer.

### Key Objectives

- User friendly, simple, and easy to understand
- At the same time, allow for complex, advanced network configuration
- Accessible and available on all breakpoints, including a native mobile app for iOS and Android
- Scalable for both large and small networks

### Design Process

1. New feature comes in from product owner
2. Research and synthesis with users
3. Tech discovery to determine feasibility
4. UX exploration and wireframing
5. Visual design and prototype
6. Deliver to development

---

## UX / UI

### Native Mobile Experience

There is a separate experience for native iOS and Android phones, focused more on network monitoring and less on configuration. The user's mobile dashboard gives them a high-level overview of the health and performance of their networks and allows them to drill deeper into specific devices to monitor and troubleshoot anything that might need their attention.

### Some Cool Features I Designed

While I worked on dozens of incremental features and updates, below are some of the larger features that involved considerable amounts of research, concepting, and design iterations.

**Custom Site Groups**
This feature gave users the ability to create custom groups of sites / devices on their network, allowing them to apply more personalized filtering when viewing activity and analytics. I created this new pattern allowing users to select or remove sites between 2 buckets to create their groups.

**Top Talkers Analytics**
One of the most common requests was for the ability for business owners to see the users or applications that were consuming the most data on their network, giving business owners insights into how they can save money and energy. I designed a new series of charts with complex filtering mechanisms allowing the user to toggle between top users and top applications data.

**Custom JLL Thresholds**
Probably the largest and most painstaking feature I worked on, this gave users the ability to set custom performance metrics for each device on their network in order to more meticulously track their performance. Prior to this, they were only able to set network-level thresholds. I designed a Mad-Libs-style side panel that guided the user through a series of inputs in order to create this new notification.

## Design System

When I started on Comcast Business's product UX team, the design system (or lack thereof) was in shambles. It had evolved from a simple marketing style guide designed by an outside agency, and never accounted for product environments. 3 products were being built when I started, each with their own design team, with their designers just making it up as they went along. Another Think designer and I took it upon ourselves to completely rethink and rebuild their UI kit, processes, and governance of the system.

### Federated Model

After some research, we determined that a federated model would be the best option for our design system. This decision was made due to the significant level of complexity involved in the structure of Comcast Business's different design departments, which are split into 3 verticals: Marketing (learn, shop experience), Product UX, and Sales.

We started by focusing just on the product UX department, which is made up of multiple products, each with their own design team. Each product would elect 1 designer to be the design system ambassador for that product, who would attend weekly governance meetings and be the sole interfacer between their product and the design system. We broke the UI kit into 3 parts:

1. **Foundations** — Contains grid/spacing, type, colors, buttons, and iconography.
2. **Shared Patterns** — Contains larger patterns like form fields, controls, navigation, modals, etc.
3. **Product-specific Pattern Libraries** — Individual libraries for each product, consisting of product-specific patterns and page templates.

### Zeroheight Storefront

We created a virtual storefront for our design system using Zeroheight, where we were able to link all our assets directly to the storefront annotations and direct downloads. This became our one-stop-shop for all information regarding how design patterns should be used as well as their behaviors, states, and associated assets.

### Governance

We created a cadence of weekly UI kit governance meetings that brought ambassadors from every product together to discuss adding, removing, or modifying patterns. The process followed a decision flow: Does the component exist? Do changes, additions, or any new work need to happen? Does it fulfill requirements? Then the governance team would discuss how to introduce the new component.

## Tools & Processes

During my time with Comcast Business Product, I discovered my passion for revolutionary design tools and the structures and processes that come with them. I was fortunate enough that CB leadership was deeply motivated to invest in new tools to enhance our design process. I led the effort to adopt Abstract to improve collaboration and version control and eventually the migration to Figma, which consolidated 4 tools into one and totally transformed how we designed.

### Abstract

The move to Abstract was an important milestone in my design career. At this point, I had become an expert in Sketch and was very excited by the prospect of adding collaboration and version control. We were among the first adopters of Abstract in the industry and would meet regularly with advocates from the company to help figure out the best way to structure our project. It wasn't until the advocates at Abstract started asking us questions about how we were using the tool that I realized just how new Abstract was, even for Abstract!

I sat down with some fellow designers and together we created and implemented a comprehensive system and process for designing within Abstract. I created detailed documentation, sent it out to the entire department, and began 1:1 onboarding sessions for each designer. I quickly became the strongest expert and advocate for Abstract in the department, regularly meeting with team members to ensure smooth onboarding, and modifying our processes and structure to fit everyone's needs.

### Figma

By now, every designer understands the value of Figma and how it has completely revolutionized the UX world. I had always been aware of Figma and would regularly play around with it to see if it was ready for full-scale adoption. It wasn't until Summer 2020, with the release of component variants and auto-layout, that I realized this thing was legit. I convinced Comcast Business Product UX leadership to, once again, take a huge risk, and allow me and the other designer on ActiveCore at the time to pilot the tool.

We got the green light in November and began a 3-month pilot, moving all of our designs to the new tool. It was a painstaking process that involved much trial and error. Not only were we fully migrating all design assets to this new tool, but we were actively learning the tool at the same time.

We finally developed a proof-of-concept, and pitched to formally adopt in January 2021. From there, Figma exploded within the Comcast Business Product UX department. Similar to Abstract, I took the lead on developing a formal process for Comcast Business designers and began 1:1 trainings. We implemented a weekly working session called Figma Fridays, where anyone was invited to come and ask questions as CB adopted the tool. Eventually, the other teams at Comcast Business took notice and began to follow suit, followed eventually by the Xfinity teams.

### Figma Onboarding Guide

I created a comprehensive onboarding guide for Comcast Business designers covering:

- **Important Terms** — Teams, Projects, Branches, Variants, Collaboration
- **Join Relevant Teams** — Teams are created for each work stream, like products, focus areas, and the design system
- **Project Structure (Products)** — Structuring projects by process (Resources, Exploration, Visual Design, Grooming, Released)
- **Project Structure (Design System)** — Foundations Libraries, Shared Pattern Libraries, Native Libraries, Product Libraries
- **File Structure (Pages)** — Cover, Exploration, Visual Design/Prototype, Components
- **Start Your Design** — Creating new files, version history
- **Branching** — Creating branches, merging, review process
- **Content Handoff** — Developer delivery system

### Developer Handoff

The delivery process gave us a new opportunity to creatively diminish the barrier between design and dev. With great power comes great responsibility. And with Figma comes the responsibility to not only create a good experience for your end users, but for non-designers entering your design files as well. I developed a system for delivering design work that included a list of remaining design actions, feature overview, table of contents, and helpful resources like guides on how to use Figma and links to the prototype and requirements info.

## The Problem

## Building the Design System

- **Section Heading:** Building the design system
- **Lead:** Meevo had never had a design system. I built one from scratch — and then convinced a skeptical engineering team to actually use it.
- **Body Paragraph 1:** I created a three-layer Figma library architecture: a Global UI Library for universal foundations (buttons, inputs, icons, tokens), a Staff-Facing Pattern Library for Appointment Book and Register patterns, and a Client-Facing Pattern Library for consumer booking and purchasing flows. The rule was simple — if a component appears in both staff and client products, it lives in Global.
- **Body Paragraph 2:** When the Director of Product expected the system to be "complete in 2 weeks," I created a readiness model that reframed the conversation. Instead of done vs. not done, we tracked five milestones per library: Usable, Consolidated, Documented, Operationalized, and Governed. Each milestone had three dimensions — design usable, dev usable, and product usable. It gave stakeholders a clear finish line without pretending a design system stops evolving.
- **Callout:** "A design system is never done — it reaches states of readiness."

## Transforming Design–Engineering Collaboration

- **Section Heading:** Transforming design–engineering collaboration
- **Lead:** When I started, Meevo's dev teams barely used Figma and resisted UX involvement. By the end, I was invited into their Microsoft Teams developer space — something unprecedented for design.

I connected Meevo developers with our tech lead, advocated for developer presence at every step of the design process, and used my access to their Teams workspace to provide pre-QA design feedback — reducing friction before it reached formal review. 6. **Design-dev bridge** — Positioned the design system as the AI pipeline: Figma → Storybook → AI-assisted development. Structured assets so AI could consume them reliably — quality in, quality out.

# Newell Brands

---

## Quick Facts

- **Timeframe:** January 2022 – June 2022
- **Company:** Newell Brands
- **Team:** 1 Design Lead, 1 Principal UX, 1 Tech Lead
- **My Role:** Principal Experience Designer
- **Tools:** Figma, Sketch, Storybook, Airtable
- **Key deliverables:** Figma training videos, white-label design system, UI screens

---

## Project Overview

Newell Brands, a global company managing over 300 eCommerce brands — including Marmot, Yankee Candle, and Elmer's Glue — was transitioning from Sketch and Zeplin to Figma to streamline their design and development workflows. After reading a blog post I had written on Think Company's website about migrating Comcast Business's product team to Figma, Newell reached out to enlist our expertise. This project was an exciting opportunity for Think to expand its service offerings. Over a 3-month engagement, I worked alongside a design and tech lead to train Newell's design team, migrate their existing assets, and refine their design-to-development process while helping redesign key product pages.

[View blog post](https://www.thinkcompany.com/blog/how-we-transitioned-a-fortune-100-companys-product-design-team-to-figma-and-lived-to-talk-about-it/)

---

## Figma Training and Migration

Newell's design team needed a structured approach to transition from their fragmented workflow to a collaborative, scalable system in Figma. Our Think team — comprising two design leads, a tech lead, and a project manager — set out to understand their existing processes, pain points, and requirements before implementing the migration.

### Key Challenges

**Managing a complex design ecosystem**
Newell's "white label" design library housed unbranded templates that were later customized per brand, requiring a seamless way to manage both white-label and branded content in Figma.

**Elevating the team's Figma proficiency**
Designers needed to become confident in Figma's tools and workflows to maintain efficiency.

**Optimizing design-to-development process**
The existing workflow lacked cohesion and efficiency, leading to misalignment between teams.

### Training

After conducting research and discovery sessions, we launched an intensive training program:

**Design team training**
Over two weeks, I led daily 2-hour workshops with Newell's core design team, covering Figma's features, including frames, auto layout, and components. I also produced eight instructional videos detailing complex functionalities for ongoing reference.

**Dev team training**
Once the designers were proficient, we introduced the development team to Figma, guiding them through best practices for design handoff.

**Storybook + Figma**
Upon learning that Newell's dev team used Storybook, our tech lead identified a plugin that allowed developers to view live Figma files directly within Storybook, improving alignment between design and code.

**Migration**
To accelerate adoption, I imported foundational components (colors, typography, grid, buttons, and icons) into Figma. The design team, under our guidance, then built out the remaining components, reinforcing their learning while developing a file structure that fostered collaboration.

---

## UX / UI

The Newell team decided to use this opportunity to redesign their white label product catalog and product detail pages, of which they had just completed a large amount of UX research in order to determine potential layout and feature enhancements. Once the design team understood Figma at an intermediate level, we put their new skills to the test as we helped them create and deliver a redesigned version of these 2 pages. We completed the design and delivery in two 2-week sprints, and were able to develop a full end-to-end process within that timeframe.

### Product Catalog Page

The first page we worked on together was the product catalog page, which displays a list of products along with some sorting and filtering options. This involved a number of enhancements including a simplified product tile which more significantly highlights the thumbnail, along with an updated horizontal filter pattern.

### Product Detail Page

Once we delivered the product catalog page, we began working on the product detail page, focusing on emphasizing the product imagery, designing a new zoom behavior, simplifying the inputs, and enhancing the content below the fold.

---

## Outcomes

- Successful migration — Newell's design team fully transitioned to Figma, improving collaboration and efficiency across teams.
- Established a scalable design system — White label templates and branded content were effectively managed within Figma, ensuring consistency across hundreds of brands.
- Seamless development integration — With Storybook and Figma connected, the design-to-development handoff process was significantly improved.
- Empowered designers — The design team developed an intermediate to expert-level understanding of Figma, enabling them to maintain and evolve their design processes independently.
- Validated design workflow — The product catalog and detail page redesigns provided an immediate, tangible application of their new skills.

---

## Reflections

This project was an exciting opportunity to help a global brand modernize their design workflows and elevate their UX maturity. Leading the Figma migration, training, and redesign efforts reinforced the importance of structured, hands-on learning and collaborative problem-solving. Watching Newell's designers gain confidence and take ownership of their new system was one of the most rewarding aspects of the engagement. By the end of our partnership, Newell's team was well-equipped to maintain and evolve their design ecosystem, ensuring continued success long after our work was complete.

# Payor Portal

---

## Quick Facts

- **Timeframe:** April 2023 – March 2024
- **Company:** Comcast
- **Team:** 1 design lead, 1 Principal UX, 4 designers, product owners, engineers
- **My Role:** Principal Experience Designer
- **Tools:** Figma, Jira, Confluence, Airtable
- **Key deliverables:** UI Kit, design documentation, multiple feature releases, process improvements

---

## Project Overview

In 2020, Merck approached Think to design a new portal to allow their Payors (health care professionals and administrators or HCPs) to safely and securely access content containing important information related to their drugs. Up until now, this process was extremely complex and not secure. When a Payor needed information on a drug that Merck developed, they would have to reach out to a Merck representative, who would then set up a time to present the content to the Payor, and then follow-up by emailing that content to the Payor.

The fully Think-staffed team was about 6 months into design when I joined the project. I immediately jumped in and took the lead on migrating them to the newly established Merck design system (Vivid360) and to Figma as their primary design tool, while assisting in the UX design, legal review, and delivery of the portal.

---

## UX / UI

### Merck Formulary Resources Portal

The Payor Portal (officially named the Merck Formulary Resources Portal) is a multi-year, dedicated portal design project, fully staffed by Think. It consists of a design lead, 2 designers, a content strategist, a tech lead, and a project manager.

**Overview**
Payor Portal is a secure content request portal that allows health care providers (HCPs) to quickly browse, request, and obtain secure and private resources related to Merck's products. There is also an experience dedicated to their scientific and pipeline information that must live separately.

**The Challenge**
Find a way to allow HCPs to access product and scientific resources in a way that is secure, is easy to use, and adheres to incredibly strict legal limitations. Because this will be tediously reviewed by the FDA, the legal parameters create incredibly challenging hurdles that threaten the overall user experience.

**Key Objectives**

- The portal must be user-friendly, easy to browse and request resources from, and secure
- Users should be able to save resources they have been granted access to
- Include all safety and indication information anywhere there is branded content that makes any kind of statement about what the drug is expected to do
- Separate branded content from scientific information

[View prototype](https://www.figma.com/proto/ByHgU6mkcInl3Xo3jDH3ow/%F0%9F%8E%A8-1.0-MFR-Experience?page-id=0%3A1637&node-id=1766%3A67336&viewport=1259%2C603%2C0.04&scaling=scale-down&starting-point-node-id=1766%3A67336&show-proto-sidebar=1)

### Design Process

1. Initial research & discovery (3rd party)
2. Feature prioritization, content model, OOUX
3. UX exploration and wireframing
4. Visual design and prototype
5. Design and legal reviews
6. Tech discovery in tandem with design
7. Deliver to dev partners
8. QA and testing

### Specific Areas I Designed

While owning design system adherence and Figma migration, I also supported the design process, alongside another designer. We each took on entire sections of the portal, some of which are highlighted below.

**Library & Requests**
The backbone of the Payor Portal is the ability for HCPs to bookmark, access, and request resources. The library and requests pages needed to adhere to strict legal guidelines, including keeping all branded content separated by brand. Once a user lands on the library page for a specific brand, they can view their activity, saved resources, and track all requests for that brand.

**Dashboard**
The dashboard is the first page the user sees once they've completed their eligibility and registration and have logged on. Many months of working sessions, testing, and legal reviews went into curating this page to provide the optimal content to the Payor at the forefront of the portal. They are able to see their library, upcoming appointments, active requests, and contacts at a glance.

---

## Design System

Merck is made up of many digital products, all of which need to closely adhere to Merck's branding. Think Company was hired by Merck in 2019 to begin the process of auditing, cataloging, and streamlining all the components that make up their digital ecosystem into a scalable, usable design system, called Vivid360. Payor was the first product to be built after the creation of the V360 system. With my previous expertise in managing and implementing design systems at Comcast Business, Think decided to bring me onto the Payor team to help the team adopt and implement the Vivid360 team's work for the first time in a Merck product.

### Migrating to Figma

The Vivid360 design system was about 85% complete when I started on Payor, but it all lived in Sketch Cloud, since that was still the primary design tool being used by Merck. Thankfully, we got authorization to use Figma for the Payor Portal. I worked independently to migrate the entire Vivid360 design system into Figma, rebuilding each component to properly utilize Figma features like auto layout and component variants.

### Design System Adherence

As the visual designer and the most senior designer on the project, I was responsible first and foremost to make sure our designs adhered to the latest release of the Vivid360 design system. Once the UI kit was fully migrated into Figma, I worked with the other designer on the team to redesign all the existing pages so that they aligned to the Vivid360 system.

---

## Tools & Processes

As previously mentioned, Sketch was the primary design tool at Merck when I joined the Payor team; they had already created the initial designs and prototype using Sketch and Invision. I proposed migrating to Figma and was able to get approval from our stakeholders. This coincided with the Vivid360 adherence, becoming an opportunity to both redesign the portal with V360 components and migrate to Figma.

### Finding the Right *Project* Structure

Finding the perfect way to organize your files in Figma is a process. It's important to allow for seamless design work that can transition between phases and designers without missing a beat. I used projects on the Figma dashboard to separate our files by phases of design, enabling designers to easily move files between the projects as they moved through the design process from exploration to delivery.

### Finding the Right *File* Structure

The file structure proved to be another difficult challenge to tackle. I wanted the main design file to work as a Payor pattern library, with all pages and elements transformed into components that would be shareable throughout the rest of the Merck ecosystem. I settled on the concept of "base pages," where all the main design screens lived. By turning these into components, I was then able to place instances of those components into the prototype page, the legal review file, and eventually the delivery file, allowing us to keep all our main design assets in one single location.

### Object Oriented UX Workshop

Working with a brilliant content strategist is a game changer, especially on such a content-heavy project, but I realized early on that we weren't quite speaking the same language. With the content model living primarily in a spreadsheet and the designs living in Figma, we were both feeling intimidated whenever trying to decipher the other's work. We decided to run an OOUX workshop with our team and develop a visualized mapping of the content model. This allowed us to understand the content as objects in the user's journey, all of which have behaviors, metadata, attributes, CTAs, and nested objects.

### RRT Reviews

One of the major aspects of this project is the considerable amount of legal limitations. When working in a pharma environment, the FDA needs to see everything. So before it comes to that, Merck uses their rapid response teams (RRT) of legal professionals to meet with designers for weekly reviews to make sure there are no legal issues with the designs. This creates a very complex additional challenge. Not only are we trying to create the best user experience, but we are also responsible for complying with very strict legal restrictions, which most of the time are at the expense of the end user. These reviews can be tedious and difficult, and fairly often result in full-scale redesigns of entire pages in order to meet compliance.

### Developer Handoff

When it came time to deliver our work, I grabbed the same system I had developed over at Comcast Business, and improved upon it. Along with a comprehensive table of contents and resources, I provided an overview of the design team with profile cards, and added even more comprehensive annotations with a global components section.

# Xfinity Point of Sale

---

## Quick Facts

- **Timeframe:** October 2024 – Present
- **Company:** Comcast
- **Team:** 2 UX designers, 1 project manager, 1 product owner, business stakeholders, engineering partners
- **My Role:** Design Lead
- **Tools:** Figma, Jira, Confluence
- **Key deliverables:** End to end UX for POS system

---

## Project Overview

For years, Comcast's retail stores relied on XStore, a slow, buggy, and inefficient point-of-sale system outsourced by Oracle. Costing millions annually, XStore's poor design and usability issues made it a bottleneck in Comcast's retail operations. To eliminate reliance on Oracle and take control of its in-store sales experience, Comcast initiated the Point of Sale (Point of Sale) project—a ground-up product design that would align with the Celestial Design System while existing as a standalone product.

I was brought in as a Principal UX Designer due to my deep knowledge of the Celestial Design System, and within weeks, I stepped up as the Design Lead after the sudden departure of the previous design manager. Under an exceptionally aggressive timeline, I led the design team in creating an entirely new POS system, ensuring we not only met the goal of operational parody with XStore, but seizing every opportunity to vastly improve the overall user experience.

---

## Problem

XStore was an expensive, inefficient, and poorly designed third-party POS system that hindered Comcast's retail operations. With the Oracle contract renewal looming in November 2025, Comcast needed to replace XStore with an in-house solution before the deadline. However, the project faced significant roadblocks:

- Business requirements were only 20% complete
- The design team was under-resourced, and two weeks into my involvement, the design manager left, creating a leadership vacuum.

Given the aggressive timeline and lack of structure, there was a serious risk that Point of Sale would not be design-ready by the end of Q1 2025.

---

## Process

Designing and delivering Point of Sale under such a high-stakes and aggressive timeline required a structured yet adaptive approach. With business requirements incomplete and leadership gaps in place, I quickly took charge of establishing a roadmap, structuring design sprints, and ensuring alignment across product, engineering, and research teams. Our process was broken down into four key phases, each aimed at driving clarity, efficiency, and delivering a best-in-class point-of-sale experience.

### Phase 1: Establishing leadership and structure

- Stepped into the interim Design Lead role after the departure of the design manager, aligning with Frontline UX leadership to formalize my leadership position.
- Implemented sprint-based meeting cadences, including sprint planning, reviews, and structured handoffs to maintain momentum and accountability.
- Collaborated closely with the product owner to finalize business requirements and estimate a roadmap that aligned with the aggressive design timeline.

### Phase 2: Designing the POS system from the ground up

- Partnered with the other designer to build the entire Point of Sale experience from scratch, using Celestial Design System components where applicable while ensuring Point of Sale functioned as an independent product.
- Created detailed information architecture, user flows, and wireframes to define the POS experience.
- Iterated rapidly through high-fidelity visual design, improving usability and efficiency beyond XStore's baseline functionality.

[View prototype](https://www.figma.com/proto/IvTI8QQgQAbzcfkjKN6vgZ/CPOS-_-%F0%9F%9A%80-Deliverables?page-id=5286%3A43655&node-id=5286-46745&viewport=1627%2C2141%2C0.54&t=C72UFOuH4rGQxLCJ-1&scaling=scale-down&content-scaling=fixed&starting-point-node-id=5286%3A46745&show-proto-sidebar=1)

### Phase 3: Integrating user research and design validation

- Brought on a UX researcher to conduct in-depth interviews (IDIs) with Celestial designers and in-store agents to validate design decisions.
- Incorporated research insights into iterative improvements, refining workflows to ensure real-world usability and alignment with retail employee needs.
- Explored Point of Sale analogs to determine industry standards and best practices.

#### Research Strategy — Our Research Goals

**Find areas for improvement**

- How intuitive do users find the prototype's interface and features? Are there any areas where they experience confusion or difficulty?
- What aspects of the prototype do users find most valuable or helpful? Which ones do they think need further refinement to enhance their overall experience?

**Identify remaining gaps**

- How can this new experience help them? Are there any other issues that need to be addressed within the experience?
- What limitations do they have if any?

### Phase 4: Executing a high-stakes, fast-paced delivery

- Worked in accelerated two-week sprints, delivering major components of the IA and UX in each cycle.
- Maintained alignment with engineering teams to ensure seamless handoff and feasibility of designs.
- On track to finish ahead of schedule, ensuring all necessary assets, documentation, and handoff materials are finalized, keeping Point of Sale on track for the development phase.

---

## Outcomes

- Successfully led the Point of Sale design team through an extremely tight turnaround, ensuring the project remained on schedule.
- Established a structured and efficient design process, improving communication and collaboration across design, product, and engineering.
- Delivered a high-quality, fully designed POS system that not only met the functional requirements of XStore but vastly improved upon its usability, scalability, and visual design.
- Played a pivotal role in keeping Comcast on track to eliminate their reliance on Oracle, saving millions annually in licensing costs.
- This project directly led to my promotion to Design Lead at Think Company in January 2025.

---

## Reflections

Point of Sale was one of the most high-stakes, fast-paced projects I have ever worked on, pushing me to take on a lead role and successfully structure the design process, align stakeholders, and deliver a world-class POS experience under immense pressure. Beyond design execution, this project was a hands-on lesson in leadership—setting clear goals, fostering collaboration, and guiding a team through tight deadlines without sacrificing quality. It not only shaped the future of Comcast's retail operations but also solidified my growth into a formal design leadership position, proving my ability to navigate complex challenges and drive strategic success.

# The Philadelphia Inquirer

---

## Quick Facts

- **Timeframe:** July 2022 – April 2023
- **Company:** Philadelphia Inquirer
- **My Role:** Product Design Lead
- **Tools:** Figma, Storybook, Tailwind CSS, Jira, Confluence, Airtable
- **Key deliverables:** Design process development, Figma migration, design system creation, designer mentorship, CTA audit

---

## Project Overview

The Philadelphia Inquirer is one of the oldest and most respected newspapers in the United States, evolving from a traditional print publication to a modern digital news platform. As the media industry transitioned from physical newspapers to digital products, The Inquirer sought to invest heavily in user experience and product design to modernize their digital presence. In July 2022, I joined as a Product Design Lead, tasked with building a structured UX process, establishing a design system, and mentoring a team of designers to elevate the overall UX maturity of the organization.

---

## Problem Statement

The Inquirer's digital experience lacked a structured UX approach, leading to inconsistencies in design, inefficient workflows, and misalignment between design and development. The design team primarily came from graphic design and marketing backgrounds with minimal UX experience, making it difficult to implement research-backed, user-centered design. Additionally, the advertising-heavy revenue model negatively impacted the user experience, with intrusive CTAs and dark patterns disrupting content consumption. Without a systematic way to evaluate these interruptions, the product and advertising teams had little alignment on balancing business needs with usability.

---

## Process

Each initiative addressed critical gaps in design workflow, consistency, and user experience, ensuring that both the design team and cross-functional partners could operate more efficiently and collaboratively.

### Establishing a UX Process and Figma Migration

**Challenges**

- The team lacked a defined design process, leading to inconsistent workflows and inefficient handoffs.
- The team's design assets were fragmented across different tools, making collaboration difficult.

**Solutions**

- I developed a structured UX process that incorporated research, discovery, user flows, wireframes, visual design, and comprehensive delivery documentation.
- Led the migration of all design assets to Figma, ensuring a single source of truth for design work and improving collaboration.

**Impact**

- Increased efficiency, improved cross-functional communication, and established consistency across design projects.

### Building a Scalable Design System

**Challenges**

- The Inquirer lacked a cohesive design system, leading to inconsistencies in UI components and difficulties in development.
- Designers had limited experience working within a systemized approach to UI design.

**Solutions**

- Created a comprehensive design system, working closely with developers to integrate Storybook and Tailwind CSS for a seamless design-to-development workflow.
- Provided training and documentation to ensure designers understood how to leverage the design system effectively.

**Impact**

- Standardized UI components, improved developer collaboration, and reduced time spent on repetitive design tasks.

### CTA Audit and Balancing UX with Ad Revenue

**Challenges**

- The product experience was frequently disrupted by excessive CTAs and advertisements, negatively impacting user engagement.
- No clear framework existed to evaluate the severity of ad interruptions.

**Solutions**

- Partnered with the research and ad teams to conduct a full audit of all CTAs and interruptions across the product.
- Created a scoring mechanism to measure the impact of different ad types, prototyping experiences to illustrate their disruption level.

---

## Outcomes

- Successfully migrated all design assets to Figma and implemented a structured UX workflow, significantly improving efficiency and team collaboration.
- Designed and implemented a scalable design system, reducing inconsistencies and improving design-to-development handoffs.
- Established a mentorship program, providing regular 1:1s and career guidance for designers transitioning into UX roles.
- Conducted a full CTA audit, creating a formal scoring model that allowed the advertising team to consider user experience in their strategies.
- Fostered a meaningful collaboration between the product and advertising teams, bridging the gap between UX and revenue generation.

---

## Reflections

My time at The Philadelphia Inquirer was a unique challenge, as it required balancing UX best practices with business needs in an industry that relies heavily on advertising revenue. I developed critical leadership skills, from mentoring a team with limited UX experience to aligning cross-functional teams around shared goals. The experience reinforced the importance of structured design systems, thoughtful process development, and the ability to advocate for user experience in environments where business priorities may not always align. It was a rewarding opportunity to influence a major news organization's digital transformation and bridge the gap between UX and marketing in a meaningful way.

# xm360

---

## Quick Facts

- **Timeframe:** April 2023 – March 2024
- **Company:** Comcast
- **Team:** 1 design lead, 5 designers, 1 copy writer, product owners, engineers
- **My Role:** Principal Experience Designer
- **Tools:** Figma, Jira, Confluence, Airtable
- **Key deliverables:** UI Kit, design documentation, multiple feature releases, process improvements

---

## Project Overview

xm360 (Xfinity Mobile 360) is the primary tool used by Xfinity retail store employees to activate and manage Xfinity Mobile customer accounts. As the only legacy product within Comcast's Frontline suite that generates revenue, xm360 is a top-priority initiative, making it one of the most fast-paced and high-stakes projects within the company. My role as Principal UX Designer involved managing the daily design operations of a four-person team while directly supporting the lead designer. Over my year on this project, I introduced structure and efficiency, improved workflows, and delivered impactful design solutions that enhanced the overall usability of xm360.

---

## Problem Statement

When I joined the XM360 team, the design process was chaotic, lacking clear sources of truth, streamlined workflows, and cohesive UI consistency. The fast-paced nature of the project meant that designers often worked in silos, leading to inconsistencies and inefficiencies in the product's experience. Additionally, there was no well-defined UI kit or centralized design documentation, making collaboration and scalability difficult.

---

## Accomplishments

Designing for XM360 required balancing efficiency, consistency, and collaboration in a fast-paced, high-stakes environment. With outdated assets, a fragmented workflow, and shifting priorities, I focused on three key areas:

1. Establishing a UI Kit and Source of Truth
2. Streamlining Workflows and Collaboration
3. Delivering High-Impact Features

Each of these initiatives helped bring structure to the design process while ensuring long-term compatibility with the Celestial Design System.

### Establishing a UI Kit and Source of Truth

**Challenges**

- XM360's design assets were outdated, and there was no structured UI documentation.
- The product would eventually transition to Celestial, requiring forward compatibility.

**Solutions**

- Developed a UI kit that standardized components, improving consistency and efficiency across the team. Created a centralized source of truth in Figma to minimize redundancy and misalignment.
- Collaborated with the Celestial team to structure assets in a way that would facilitate a smooth transition.

### Streamlining Workflows and Collaboration

**Challenges**

- The team operated in a chaotic environment with frequent pivots and shifting priorities.
- The design lead was polarizing and difficult, causing friction within the team.
- Product owners were often absent or lacked direction.

**Solutions**

- Implemented structured workflows, design review cycles, and collaborative feedback loops to ensure alignment.
- Acted as a buffer between the lead and designers, fostering trust and cohesiveness across the team.
- Took on additional responsibilities traditionally managed by product owners to ensure the project moved forward.

### Delivering High-Impact Features

**Challenges**

- The product's fast pace meant little time for iteration, leading to rushed design decisions.
- The lack of clear handoffs created miscommunication between design and engineering.

**Solutions**

- Established structured processes for validation and iteration, ensuring that design quality was upheld while meeting tight deadlines.
- Improved documentation and alignment sessions, ensuring designs were implemented accurately.

---

## Outcomes

- Standardized UI components, increasing efficiency and consistency across the design team.
- Reduced inconsistencies in XM360's UI, improving usability for retail employees.
- Strengthened collaboration between design, product, and engineering, leading to more seamless product iterations and faster development cycles.
- Established a structured transition plan for XM360's eventual migration to Celestial.

---

## Reflections

XM360 was a demanding yet rewarding project that tested my ability to drive meaningful change in a high-pressure environment. By introducing structure, improving collaboration, and ensuring long-term scalability, I helped set the foundation for a more efficient and cohesive design system. This experience reinforced my expertise in building scalable design solutions while navigating complex team dynamics.

## Specific accomplishments


Individual pieces of work, with what he did and what changed as a result.


### Reset the Meevo design system's color token naming to usage-based semantics — August 2026
- **What:** Meevo's design system engineer flagged that the existing color token names would not survive dark mode — the naming was tied to values rather than to how the colors are used, so an inverted scale would make every name a lie. Researched how semantic token naming has cycled across the industry (usage-based → value-based → back to usage-based as dark mode became standard), then duplicated the Figma global library, renamed every color variable to the usage-based convention, took Meevo's design system engineer's review, and published to the **main global library**.
- **Impact:** The rename is a precondition for dark mode shipping at all. An inverted dark scale under value-based names produces tokens whose names contradict their values, which is the failure mode that makes a design system stop being trusted. Doing it before the dark mode implementation meant a variable rename in Figma rather than a coordinated rename across Figma, Prism and every consuming deliverable.
- **Role:** led
- **Skills:** design systems, design tokens, semantic naming, standards research, Figma variables

### Set the Meevo design system's global touch-target standard at AA — August 2026
- **What:** Following a gap **the account's second design lead** raised in the 2026-08-11 catch-up — target sizing documented inconsistently across individual deliverables with no global rule anywhere — researched WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 and 2.5.5 and how six other design systems document target size (Primer, Spectrum, Material 3, Carbon, USWDS, GOV.UK), measured all **20 default-state variants** of the `Button` component set, and wrote a **single global foundations rule** rather than per-component numbers. Published three ways: the Notion ticket, the Figma Global UI Library foundations page, and a standalone redlined HTML spec with eight annotated plates built from the live design tokens.
- **Role:** led
- **Skills:** accessibility (WCAG 2.2 AA), design systems, design-system documentation, standards research, technical writing, AI enablement

### Delivered the Figma-vs-Prism discrepancy audit with no code access — August 2026
- **What:** Scoped, built and delivered *State of the Design System at Handoff* — a 34-finding audit comparing Meevo's three Figma libraries against the Prism code design system, shared with **Meevo's design system owner** (who now owns all design system work under Meevo's new CTO) and **Meevo's design system engineer** via [Aha M20-888](https://millenniumsi.aha.io/features/M20-888). Built entirely from the **public Storybook build and the live Figma files — no repository access**, after Meevo's design system owner declined to share source code. Shipped as a standalone HTML document plus a machine-readable findings register (CSV + JSON), because Meevo's design system owner intended to feed it to his own AI tooling.
- **Role:** led
- **Skills:** design systems, technical auditing, design tokens, AI-assisted analysis, technical writing for mixed audiences

### Ran the dark mode accessibility audit that caught what manual review had missed — August 2026
- **What:** Stress-tested dark mode across **every Appointment Book screen pattern** rather than spot-checking — basic calendar, mobile, mobile landscape, user-defined colors, yield management, clock-in indicators, employee masking, work activities, single- and multi-guest booking, summary panel, view appointment. Used Claude to run the contrast comparisons, then drove every finding to a decision in the client review and produced a change log spanning **two systems** (Prism plus a Meevo engineer's dashboard/Quicksite).
- **Impact:** Found real defects nobody had noticed in months of manual work: **form field borders failing WCAG AA** against the light gray background (a rule the team didn't know applied), the **unconfirmed yellow misused in the Utilization Bar** alongside system colors — invisible on mobile full-screen where the text label drops — and the **confirmed status border at 2.99 contrast**. All three were caught *before* Meevo implemented dark mode in Prism, which is the difference between a design fix and a code migration.
